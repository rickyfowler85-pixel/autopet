## 2. Guiding design philosophy

Autopet remains grounded in its established principle:

**A little life that unfolds with or without you, but is uniquely shaped by you.**

The experience is:

- Passive by default, interactive by choice.
- Emotionally engaging without demanding attention.
- Visually simple, with sophisticated underlying simulation.
- Driven by the creature's autonomous decisions.
- Influenced by the player without being controlled by them.
- Continuously developmental rather than organized around fixed progression milestones.

The habitat should feel like a genuine little place that the creature inhabits, not a collection of game systems the player manages.

**Core environmental principle: The player provides opportunities. The creature decides how to engage with them.**

## 3. Approved V1 habitat foundation

### Environmental composition

The initial habitat is a fixed, single-screen natural clearing with a shallow three-quarter perspective.

Its primary elements are:

**Open central clearing**
- Natural traversable ground.
- Sufficient negative space for movement and interaction.
- Subtle vegetation and terrain variation.
- The creature remains the primary focal point.

**Permanent shallow pool**
- Integrated into one side of the habitat.
- Accessible from the beginning.
- Supports drinking, investigating, bathing, splashing, wading, and eventually more advanced aquatic behaviors if anatomy allows.
- Water exposure alone does not guarantee aquatic development.
- No drowning hazards or survival-management requirements.

**Permanent natural shelter**
- Integrated into exposed roots or vegetation.
- Provides an accessible resting opportunity.
- Does not force the creature to sleep there.
- The creature can develop favorite resting locations and gradually make an area its own.

**Supporting natural elements**
- Vegetation, grass, roots, stones, and sparse atmospheric details.
- Familiar natural forms with subtle alien characteristics.
- No overt science-fiction architecture, artificial enclosures, or conventional constructed pet housing.

The world should feel larger than its visible boundaries while remaining a compact, self-contained V1 experience.

### Environmental ownership

The habitat belongs to the creature as much as the player.

Over time, the creature might establish favorite resting locations, move lightweight objects, return to familiar areas, or collect interesting things.

These modifications persist and become subtle evidence of the creature's individual history.

## 4. Approved environmental object system

Three initial movable object families have been established.

| Object | Properties | Potential behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| Branch | Lightweight, elongated, irregular | Carrying, dragging, climbing, balancing, playing |
| Stone | Compact, relatively heavy, stable | Pushing, climbing, resting, investigating |
| Seedpod | Lightweight, rounded, organic | Rolling, carrying, collecting, opening, investigating |

Objects have multiple affordances rather than a single predetermined purpose.

**Object placement and ownership**
- Both player and creature can reposition small objects.
- Players use freeform drag-and-release placement.
- The system silently assists with physically valid positioning.
- No visible placement grid or construction interface.
- Creature-initiated object movement respects physical and anatomical capabilities.
- Object placement persists.

**Object introduction**
- Some objects appear naturally through environmental activity.
- Players may optionally introduce selected objects.
- No inventory-management chores, resource economy, or crafting requirements.
- Object quantities remain restrained to preserve readability.

**Simple physical state changes**
- Branches may show signs of use.
- Stones may become partially submerged or otherwise change spatial context.
- Seedpods may crack or open.
- Temporary reactions are distinguished from persistent changes.

A complete ecological decay or growth simulation is not required for V1.

## 5. Feeding and natural foraging

The creature must be capable of feeding itself without player intervention.

**Everyday feeding**
- Reliable natural food availability is built into vegetation and terrain.
- Food discovery is subtle and contextual.
- The creature may sniff, investigate, rummage, uncover food, and eat.
- Food should not primarily appear as collectible icons scattered around the screen.
- Natural feeding continues autonomously while the player is away.

**Special food discoveries**
- Seedpods and potentially other objects can occasionally contain edible discoveries.
- These experiences can influence food preferences, curiosity, and foraging behavior.
- Special discoveries are not survival requirements.

**Player feeding**
- Food can be gathered directly from natural habitat sources.
- An unobtrusive contextual selector provides a convenient alternative.
- Players can offer food directly or leave it in the environment.
- The creature may accept, investigate, decline, or ignore offerings.

Direct feeding can contribute to social development, while independent discovery can reinforce foraging experience.

Dependence should emerge from repeated experience, not from a single feeding interaction.

**No feeding schedules, starvation penalties, or mandatory maintenance.**

## 6. Autonomous behavioral system

The creature develops recognizable but flexible routines.

Behavioral decisions should emerge from four interacting influences:

1. **Internal state:** Energy, comfort, mood, curiosity, and immediate needs.
2. **Learned history:** Temperament, confidence, habits, preferences, memories, and independence.
3. **Environmental opportunities:** Accessible objects, available activities, location, and physical capabilities.
4. **Spontaneous variation:** Occasional experimentation and deviations from established habits.

The creature can choose to wander, rest, forage, groom, investigate, play, manipulate objects, or simply observe.

Constant activity is not desirable. Quiet inactivity is part of the experience.

### Curiosity and unfamiliar objects

Responses depend on evolving temperament, confidence, and experience.

A creature might approach something immediately, watch from a distance, tentatively investigate, or avoid it.

These tendencies remain malleable.

### Responses to difficulty

Creatures develop individual responses to unsuccessful activities.

They may persist, hesitate, retreat, seek alternatives, or revisit an activity later.

Both successful and unsuccessful attempts contribute meaningful experience.

**Failure produces learning, not punishment.**

### Growing independence

Independence develops through experience, environmental opportunities, confidence, maturity, and patterns of care.

Attachment, dependence, and social expressiveness remain separate developmental qualities.

An affectionate creature can be highly independent; a dependent creature can still learn self-sufficiency.

## 7. Player interaction and shared play

The approved interaction model is **direct physical interaction with secondary contextual actions**.

| Player action | Intended response |
|---|---|
| Tap creature | Acknowledgment and subtle contextual affordances |
| Stroke creature | Personality-dependent petting response |
| Gentle play invitation | Voluntary creature response |
| Move environmental object | Freeform physical placement |
| Offer food | Creature may accept or decline |
| Leave food | Opportunity for later independent discovery |
| Select contextual action | Access memories and other abstract functions |

Mouse and touch interactions should both be supported, with accessible alternatives to gesture-dependent actions.

### Shared play

Players can initiate play through environmental objects or gentle direct invitations.

Play can become spontaneous back-and-forth interaction.

For example, a player rolls a seedpod toward the creature. It may investigate, chase, return, carry away, or ignore the object.

Over repeated experiences, the creature may develop familiar shared play patterns and eventually initiate them independently.

These are learned behaviors, not fixed minigame unlocks.

Play remains voluntary, interruptible, and developmentally meaningful.

### Contextual encouragement

Affection can support comfort, bonding, confidence, and voluntary experimentation.

It should never force specific behaviors or guarantee success.

The creature can accept reassurance and still decide to do something else.

---

## 8. Creature-initiated social interaction

The creature can occasionally invite the player to interact.

Possible behaviors include:

- Looking toward the player.
- Approaching affectionately.
- Presenting a favored object.
- Inviting play.
- Drawing attention toward something interesting.

These invitations are subtle, optional, and personality-dependent.

### Adaptive social behavior

The creature gradually learns patterns in how the player engages.

A highly interactive player may encourage more expressive social invitations.

A largely observational player may develop a creature that offers quieter acknowledgments while remaining content with autonomous activities.

**Less interaction does not mean less affection.**

Unanswered invitations never generate guilt, punitive decline, or an obligation to return.

## 9. Offline progression and memories

Autopet continues developing while the player is away.

Autonomous progression includes:

- Exploration and foraging.
- Repeated activity and skill development.
- Formation of habits and preferences.
- Environmental object movement.
- Discoveries and behavioral learning.
- Gradual physical and developmental changes.

Active participation provides greater influence and direct observation, not intrinsically superior progression.

For implementation, offline time can be processed through simulation catch-up rather than requiring a continuously running application.

### Environmental storytelling

The player should notice changes through the habitat and creature itself.

Examples include a branch that has moved, an opened seedpod, a new favorite resting location, or improved balancing behavior.

### Optional memories

The creature can surface short observations of actual experiences, discoveries, and developing habits.

Memories are accessed **through interaction with the creature**, not a separate journal object or persistent toolbar button.

The habitat opens normally without forced recaps.

Memory content must be grounded in recorded simulation events and avoid revealing every internal statistic.

These same recorded experiences can support the established Evolution Reflection mechanic.

---

## 10. Procedural evolution integration

Environmental activity provides evidence for behavioral and anatomical development.

Examples:

- Climbing and balancing may influence grip, coordination, posture, and relevant anatomy.
- Water activity may contribute to aquatic adaptations.
- Manipulating heavier objects may contribute to strength and physical capability.
- Foraging may influence exploration, learned proficiency, and independence.
- Social interaction may influence attachment, confidence, and learned dependence.

Relationships are probabilistic and constrained by existing anatomy and developmental history.

Objects do not directly award evolution points or prescribe fixed evolutionary forms.

Major metamorphoses remain governed by the broader Evolution Reflection system, including the player's optional interpretation of the creature's actual experiences.

The environmental simulation should record meaningful experiences in a structure that other developmental systems can consume.

### Reference 02 — Habitat and creature integration

A second concept develops the same habitat with a blue/slate creature and supplementary animation and asset studies.

It demonstrates:

- Cohesive creature-to-environment styling.
- Readable movement and behavioral poses.
- Environmental affordances.
- Cohesive pixel-art treatment.
- Potential reusable behavior animations.

**Both images were positively received and approved as the visual foundation.**

### Production considerations

The integrated creature still needs to be reconciled with the separately approved canonical juvenile anatomy.

In particular:
- Preserve the original compact quadrupedal proportions.
- Maintain restrained ear, dorsal ridge, and tail structures.
- Avoid allowing the creature to drift toward a conventional fox or exaggerated fantasy creature.
- Preserve readable expressions and silhouettes.

Environmental density must also be reduced or refined as needed for small-display readability.

The approved images are mood, composition, and behavior references—not finalized sprite atlases or implementation specifications.

The creature does not have an official species or character name. Do not reintroduce discarded anatomical working names as public-facing identities.

## 12. V1 scope and exclusions

The approved direction deliberately avoids:

- Hunger or starvation penalties.
- Player-maintained care schedules.
- Resource collection economies.
- Crafting and base construction.
- Complex weather or hazard systems.
- Multiple biome management.
- Fixed behavioral training commands.
- Traditional minigames.
- Mandatory notifications or welcome-back recaps.
- Performance dashboards or achievement grinding.
- Large environmental inventories.

The sophistication should reside in creature simulation and emergent interaction—not in visible management complexity.

## 13. Recommended next milestone: Playable Habitat Prototype 01

The next step should be a deliberately limited interactive prototype.

**Primary objective:** Determine whether the creature feels alive, autonomous, responsive, and emotionally engaging within the approved habitat.

### Proposed prototype scope

**Visual layer**
- One simplified, single-screen habitat.
- Integrated root shelter and shallow pool.
- Canonical juvenile creature sprite.
- Representative branch, stone, and seedpod.
- Small set of coherent animation states.

**Creature behavior**
- Autonomous idle, wandering, resting, and exploration.
- Elementary curiosity and object investigation.
- Simple temperament-driven behavior selection.
- Activity preference tracking.
- Basic reaction to successful or unsuccessful interactions.

**Player interaction**
- Tap acknowledgment.
- Petting.
- Object repositioning.
- Simple food offering.
- Basic play invitation and response.

**Simulation**
- Persist creature and environment state.
- Record meaningful activity events.
- Support accelerated simulation time.
- Demonstrate offline progression through controlled tests.
- Support basic memory observations.

The prototype need not implement complete procedural anatomy, full metamorphosis, every behavioral nuance, or the final product interface.

It should, however, be designed so these systems can be integrated later without requiring a complete rewrite.

### What the prototype must demonstrate

1. Does the creature appear to make its own decisions?
2. Does interacting with it feel natural rather than command-driven?
3. Can different behavioral tendencies be meaningfully observed?
4. Can it use the same environmental object in multiple ways?
5. Do player interactions influence behavior without directly controlling it?
6. Can meaningful autonomous progression occur while the player is absent?
7. Does the art remain expressive and readable at the intended scale?
8. Is observing the creature enjoyable even without interacting?
